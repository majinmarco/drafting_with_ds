The Projections, the Market, and the Curves

Pull ESPN's own 2026 projections and draft-market data into pandas, and watch Lesson 1's scarcity story appear in the real numbers.

In Lesson 1 you learned that value is relative to replacement level, using an illustrative table. Today you get the real thing: actual 2026 data, from the same API that powers your own ESPN league. Two datasets matter, and the difference between them is where your entire edge lives:

1 · Projections — what players are worth

A projection is a model's estimate of a player's total fantasy points this season. ESPN publishes weekly projections per player; summing weeks 1–18 gives a season projection in your league's exact scoring. Projections are the input to every value calculation we'll do.

2 · ADP — what players cost

Average Draft Position (ADP) is the average pick number at which a player is being drafted across thousands of real drafts. It is the market price. A player with ADP 25.3 typically goes late in round 2. Crucially, ADP tells you what your 11 opponents will probably do — which is what lets you predict who'll still be available at your next pick.

Projections are value; ADP is price. Drafting well means buying value for less than the market price — and the market's mistakes are visible only when you hold both datasets side by side.

Your task (~20 minutes)

The notebook pulls from ESPN's fantasy API (leaguedefaults/3 = the default PPR settings your league uses), flattens the JSON into a tidy dataframe of name, pos, proj, adp, and saves ~390 players to data/espn_players_2026.csv. You should see Jahmyr Gibbs, Bijan Robinson, and Ja'Marr Chase at the top of the ADP order. Then complete its three TODO cells (positional rank, scarcity-curve plot, last-starter baselines) — check cells re-grade your work automatically as you type, and solutions are in an accordion at the bottom. You know all the pandas needed; the point is to derive the scarcity story yourself rather than take my word for it.

This is an unofficial-but-public API; the interesting part is the JSON filtering — ESPN tags every stat line with statSourceId (0 = actual, 1 = projected) and statSplitTypeId (1 = single week). Getting good at reading a messy nested API payload is a real DS skill; this one's a nice specimen.

                    .rank(ascending=False, method="first").astype(int))
