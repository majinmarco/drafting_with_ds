The Snake Draft, and Why Value Is Always Relative

Everything you need to know about how the game works — and the one idea that separates data-driven drafters from everyone else at the table.

The game in ninety seconds

In your league, 12 people each build a roster of real NFL players. Every week of the NFL season, your roster is matched head-to-head against one other team in the league. Your players earn points for what they actually do on the field that week — yards, touchdowns, catches — and whichever of the two rosters scores more points wins the week. Best win–loss records make the playoffs; playoffs decide the champion.

Each week you choose a starting lineup from your roster; only starters score. On ESPN's default settings, that lineup is:

SlotCountMeaning
QB1Quarterback
RB2Running backs
WR2Wide receivers
TE1Tight end
FLEX1Any RB, WR, or TE
D/ST1A team's whole defense
K1Kicker
Bench~7Reserves; score nothing
ESPN's default scoring is PPR fractional: 1 point per reception, 0.1 per rushing/receiving yard, 0.04 per passing yard, 6 per rushing/receiving TD, 4 per passing TD. Verify your league's actual settings in the ESPN app (League → Settings) — everything we compute later depends on them. See ESPN's settings guide.

The draft is where you acquire your initial roster. Every real NFL player is available; once someone drafts a player, he's theirs alone. With 12 teams filling ~16 roster spots, roughly 190 players get drafted. The draft is, by a wide margin, the highest-leverage event of your season — which is why it's worth optimizing.

How a snake draft works

Teams pick in a fixed order, but the order reverses every round — hence "snake." In a 12-team league, whoever has pick 1 of round 1 gets the last pick of round 2, then the first pick of round 3, and so on. This compensates for how much better pick 1 is than pick 12.

Your pandas brain will appreciate that your pick numbers are a closed-form function of your draft slot s and round r:

pick(r, s) = (r - 1) * 12 + s          # odd rounds
pick(r, s) = r * 12 - s + 1            # even rounds

So if you draw slot 4, your first five picks are numbers 4, 21, 28, 45, 52. Notice the rhythm: after each odd-round pick you wait a long time, then get two picks close together. That gap — how many players come off the board between your picks — is the core constraint your strategy must plan around. In the middle of the snake you wait ~11 picks every time; on the turns (slots 1 and 12) you pick twice back-to-back, then wait 22.

The one idea that matters: value over replacement

Here is the mistake nearly every casual drafter makes: they rank players by projected points. By raw points, quarterbacks dominate — a top QB might project ~380 points while the best running back projects ~330. So surely you draft QBs early?

No. You aren't trying to maximize points at a position; you're trying to maximize your edge over the alternative you'd otherwise start. Every one of the 12 teams starts a QB, and the 12th-best QB still scores a lot. Running backs are different: production collapses fast as you go down the rankings, and every team needs two (plus flex). Compare typical PPR projection shapes (illustrative magnitudes):

PositionBest playerLast starter*Gap
QB (12 start)~380~320~60
RB (24+ start)~330~180~150
WR (24+ start)~340~200~140
TE (12 start)~250~140~110
*"Last starter" ≈ the QB12, RB24, WR24, TE12 — the worst player who still starts league-wide each week. Numbers are illustrative of typical PPR shapes, not 2026 projections; we'll compute the real ones from data in Lesson 3.

This is value over replacement (VOR): a player's worth is his projection minus the projection of the replacement level at his position — roughly, the best player you could get without spending a meaningful pick (the last starter, or the best player left undrafted). The elite QB is only ~60 points better than a QB you can grab ten rounds later. The elite RB is ~150 points better than his replacement. Same jersey-number points, wildly different value.

A player's value is not his points. It is his points minus the points of the player you'd start instead. Scarcity at the position, not star power, is what wins drafts.

This single reframing — from absolute to relative value — is the foundation of everything we'll build: the VOR cheat sheet, the market-gap analysis against average draft position, the simulator, the optimizer. It's also a general data-science lesson in disguise: choosing the right baseline is the modeling decision, and we'll spend a whole lesson on how to choose it.
